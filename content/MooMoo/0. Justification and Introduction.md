# Introduction
Over the last years we have seen a growing social awareness that the design of disability and
AT must become more inclusive, and that disabled people and their communities1 must be
included as active participants and recognized as experts in the making process (Costanza-
Chock, 2020; Shew, 2023). In the past, disability and AT weren’t part of the broader sociotechnological discussion, which has led to technologically centric, unusable, and abandoned
AT. This has led to the marginalization of disabled people, who then face difficulties to function
in society (Williams, 2007). Over time AT evolved, using universal design principles, towards
more inclusive and more empowering forms of AT for the disabled community (Guffey, 2023;
Williams, 2007). AT ‘making’ represents a step forward towards increased social inclusion and
empowerment by incorporating community members as expert technology creators in the design
and development processes however, oftentimes the creators can’t participate in the making
process due to external, environmental and technological ableist difficulties (Costanza-Chock,
2020; Shew, 2023).
	Braille reading technology is an example of such a problem illustrating how many instances
of universal design concepts don’t fully accommodate blind people’s needs. Historical attempts,
such as “Boston Linear Type” and AT devices such as “Optophone,” prioritized visual and
auditory text interpretation, completely ignoring blind people’s preference for tactile braille
(“The Country of the Blind,” 2023). Inventions such as these, while framed as inclusive and
accommodating, completely overlook disabled people’s voices and choice of haptic reading
methods as preferred. Despite many antecedent efforts (many of them highly problematic, due to
ableist and incorrect assumptions about the generalization of visual reading to braille reading),
braille symbols nowadays are completely haptic and standardized (“The Country of the Blind,”
2023). Because of the tactility of braille symbols, pre-school children require sensory, and motor
skills related to touch and hand movement to be able to distinguish between the embossed raised
dots (also called pins), which, when grouped together in a 6- or 8-dot grid, form diverse braille
symbols known as a ‘braille cell’ (Hilditch, 2024). The challenges of haptic-oriented learning
processes and the lack of braille letter and symbol knowledge in children’s pre and early
education curricula, provide an opportunity to explore the materiality of braille cell using DIY
methodology. Focusing on making affordability and accessibility I research and explain
difficulties of making for future open-source community members use (Barlow–Brown &
Connelly, 2002). 
	The idea of DIY open-source documentation and multimodality of interaction
focused on tactile braille letter form creates the opportunity to explore braille learning with the
focus on storytelling-based task facilitation handled by LLMs. When storytelling is integrated
into play-based activities, it serves as an effective tool for early literacy, opening up the
opportunity for exploring LLMs as a means of task facilitation (Maureen et al., 2020). Creative
engagement can explore the possibilities to ease the difficult task creation process and examine
creation to be more entertaining and engaging.

## My Own Positionality
As a university student at the undergraduate and graduate levels, I have taken numerous
design and human-computer interaction classes, and my interest in AT developed as I learned
about community members' inclusion initiatives, such as codesign and Design Justice (Costanza-
Chock, 2020). I wish to explore how AT can be used to support community members. I am not
disabled and want to avoid solutionism in my research-creation process. I feel that facilitating
technological exploration can support disabled people to learn how to make their own DIY AT.
This approach of knowledge building can help ensure that this project doesn’t fall into the trap of
being technoableist. My research doesn't aim to create a specific device that satisfies my own
physical reality, but rather, explores possibilities of DIY making through designing, creating, and
reflecting on available creation means. The design prototypes and reflection results are treated as
possible knowledge entry points which, in the future, will allow the community to modify,
extend, and use the knowledge produced as they feel is suited.

## MRP Precis
In this Major Research Project (MRP), I explore the technological possibilities of a dynamic
braille cell module designed to teach letter concepts in early education. This exploration
investigates the possibilities and limitations of DIY making, generating insights that, if taken up
by experts in the future, could enrich and expand approaches to Braille learning and technology
creation. Beginning with the understanding that learning how to interpret a braille symbol is a
cognitive process, I have explored the possibilities of storytelling task facilitation with attention
to the options afforded by LLMs. An LLM-based interactive agent gives flexibility and
engagement while learning braille by providing personalized cognitive tasks and storytelling-
based task-related creative engagement. I also researched rapid prototyping and electronic
components' pros and cons as DIY means of creation and personalization of braille AT devices.
That knowledge brings the general understanding about DIY making, highlighting the
difficulties, possibilities, and flaws.

# Background

## Solutionism
Technoableism, a concept introduced by Ashley Shew (2023), focuses on how solutionism is
often incorporated in AT for disabled people and suggests that a disability can be “cured”
through pure technological engineering. She emphasizes that environmental adjustments are
crucial to meet the needs of the disabled community, rather than using a technical tool that
compensates for environmental challenges.
	Sasha Constanza-Chock's (2020) book on Design Justice explains how systematic
inequalities have led to the marginalization of particular groups of people. They emphasized how
the “matrix of domination” relates to structural inequalities, which oftentimes are the result of
the lack of inclusion of relevant parties in the process of design. They suggest that inequities
should be challenged, not replicated, and wrote 10 design justice principles that emphasize
community-led approaches and advice to be clear about the concepts, process, recipients and
assumptions.
	The concept of technoableism and the Design Justice movement are central to my research
project because they advocate for a more equitable development process that centers on
community members and uses of technology that are available and accessible. Among many
foundational Design Justice concepts is the idea of popular education (“pop ed”) facilitation
which can be applied to LLM agent functionality. Technoableism and the Design Justice
prioritizes community engagement in the creation process which is important to my research to
focus guidelines format as a form of facilitation. Design Justice also informs that my designerly
views do not influence both the open scholarship outcomes and the task creation process
(Costanza-Chock, 2020).

## Braille Pin Exploration
Braille consists of tactile symbols that require haptic skills. Pre-learners (i.e., those who have
not yet begun intensive, tactile-oriented braille learning processes at school) are not familiar with
print and letter symbolic meaning (Barlow–Brown & Connelly, 2002). Before learning braille,
children often aren't exposed to the concept of letters (Barlow–Brown & Connelly, 2002, p. 259).
This type of learning is often considered to be a good addition to the general process of early-
stage braille learning (Barlow–Brown & Connelly, 2002, p. 268).
	Tallon and Herzberg (2013) investigated how learners acquire and apply knowledge of
contractions, which are special braille symbols representing words or grouped letters. They were
interested in the impacts of contraction knowledge on improving braille reading speed.
Conversely, they found that a lack of this knowledge can decrease braille reading speed and
consequently academic progress. In their case study, they tested a learning system of braille
contractions to improve reading speed and to improve the learning process to be more
comprehensive and faster. Their process included knowledge and identification of the braille
symbol dot placement, verbalization of separate contraction’s letters, identification of contraction
sounds and usage and decoding of the contractions (Tallon & Herzberg, 2013). Due to the
participant’s difficulties in braille symbols dot placement recognition, the early experimental
stage focused on letter form and dot placement knowledge, noting them as significant factors in
learning new symbols. The case study concluded that direct clear instructions, and systematic
formal and informal review may be an effective model for teaching braille literacy skills. The
participants improved general braille reading skills and generated motivating self-responsibility
to learn new contractions.
	Barlow-Brown and Connelly (2002) presented research about letter knowledge and
phonological awareness in young braille readers. They emphasized that letter knowledge is
connected to phonological awareness in young children in pre-school education. The research
was based, in part, on an assumption that braille reading could be treated as a version of visual
print alphabet reading (often referred to as a cross-modality assumption). The study was
performed with blind children and braille letters, and, despite the problematic cross-modality
assumption, their results did bring significant new knowledge about the influence of
phonological awareness on the acquisition of brain letter learning: children with greater braille
letter knowledge have a greater phonological awareness and that braille letter knowledge is a
major contributor to the recognition of letter phonology.
	This research is crucial to my project because it suggests that there is an opportunity to
explore avenues for enriching braille learning in pre-school and early learning setting in
conjunction with speech sounds. It emphasizes the importance of exposure to the letter concept
in early education and guides my research toward how to promote braille cell letter learning and
braille symbol phonological awareness.

## Artificial Intelligence, Storytelling, and Facilitation
Ashley Shew (2020) discussed solutionism, ableism, and technoableism in connection to AI-
driven AT such as “autonomous vehicles, companion robots (like Paro), home-based
applications (like Alexa), and caretaking robots” (Shew, 2020, p. 48). She criticized the
technological reliance on AI-driven AT and argued that artificial intelligence in the lives of
disabled or old people sometimes violates their independence. Moreover, she pointed out that
AI-based AT often focuses on accommodating caregivers rather than helping disabled people
with their needs. Shew’s main point was that, in some cases, the ‘independence’ that AI claims
to offer is a replacement for ableism. She thinks that AI should be used to facilitate the disabled
individual and to give them the same access to “autonomy rights and freedoms” as abled-bodied
individuals (Shew, 2020, p. 49).
	Shakeri et al. (2021) developed an interesting collaborative storytelling application which
usage was facilitated through AI-driven LLMs. The research focused on collaborative
storytelling to facilitate online long-distance social interaction during the COVID-19 pandemic.
To accommodate engagement among those living in different time zones, the authors developed
an asynchronous storytelling chat application called Shared Authoring GPT-3 Application
(SAGA), which autonomously created a continuation of the story between two people. SAGA
was based on the LLM GPT-3. The study, which was conducted between 2 participants,
indicated that SAGA helped to facilitate storytelling, push the plot forward and engage the user
in a discovery process. LLM has also been used to train children to have better question-asking skills and enable them to explore new topics using LLM. Abdelghani et al. (2024) did this in their study with an app based on LLM facilitation that is focused on curiosity-driven questions (so called “divergent questions”) in order to train children’s ability to ask questions and to increase their interest in different topics. To accommodate this, authors developed LLM-based app (using GPT-3) that automates the process of creating hints (called “cues”). The study compared three types of
generated cues: one type as created by humans and two other types, both generated by the LLM-
based app. The results indicated that cues generated by humans and LLM-based app were
indistinguishable. Moreover, children benefited the most when the LLM-based app had more
autonomy in open-ended question generation, indicating the facilitation via GenAI-based
learning.
	LLM's ability to facilitate tasks, especially in the educational field, presents a great
opportunity for its use in AT. Prior research in LLM has proven to be successful when used as a
task facilitator, as well as a creator of personalized outputs. Following that, LLM facilitation
through storytelling and dynamic content generation can effectively facilitate educational tasks
in braille education and AT. Although LLMs present many benefits, prior research underscores
the necessity of preserving personal autonomy. This is vital because of AI's role in tasks: it is
crucial to view AI as a supportive tool rather than fully entrusting it. These findings emphasize
that AI-driven applications must respect individual independence, and indicate that LLMs, such
as GPT, can be used more effectively to fulfill human needs and serve as a specialized tool.

## Accessibility in DIY AT making
Hook et al. (2014) conducted a study to investigate the problems and challenges of AT Do-
It-Yourself (DIY) making. To gather data, the authors conducted interviews with disabled
people, family caregivers and people closely working with the disabled community. The results
revealed that one of the main difficulties was the lack of maker's confidence, which points to the
stereotypical view that DIY AT design and construction is difficult. According to the authors,
this false assumption leads DIY makers, who are actually capable of building various ATs, to
give up before attempting construction. Participants also highlighted that, before engagement in
making, hands-on presentation provides assurance that AT was user suitable. The interview data
also showed the benefits of pre-making “endorsement from, and engagement with,
professionals”, in addition to online resources with materials and instructions (Hook et al., 2014,
p. 602). Considering the above problems, the authors emphasized rapid prototyping tools usage
are good to use when developing DIY AT.
	Herskovitz et al (2023) also emphasized the importance of disabled people having access to
AT ‘making’ and outlined that the belief that a maker’s lack of skills is an obstacle to making
and customizing ATs. They emphasize the importance of a DIY repository to use and modify by
community members and therefore formalize the ‘making’ community DIY-AT creation
practice. They also mention programming techniques to accommodate not only professionals,
but also people without programming knowledge and skills in the formation of a block-based
visual programming as a replacement for traditional coding to encourage the personalization of
AT software however underlines lack of disability accessibility in those solutions.
	Hurst and Kane (2013) point to high AT abandonment rate because of the inadequate
accommodation of disabled community members’ needs. As a solution, they emphasize AT
creation and personalization empowerment, with a focus on DIY availability and accessibility.
Hurst and Kane (2013) mention that popular online community sites such as “Make Magazine
Blog”, “Instructables”, and “Thingiverse” are good platforms to share DIY knowledge and
“encourage users to make their designs freely available to others, and encourage discussion,
questions, and feedback on the designs” (Hurst & Kane, 2013, p. 636). To make the design
process more accessible to the DIY-AT disabled makers, there is a need for the use of accessible
rapid prototyping as “personal-scale manufacturing” (Hurst & Kane, 2013, p. 636). The authors
developed two tools (VizTouch and Easy Make Oven) to support the accessible creation and
replicability of physical objects, making children engage in DIY activities. Furthermore,
reflecting on above technologies they say that through ‘making’ tools and “improving online
communities, providing improved accessibility of rapid prototyping hardware itself, and
developing more accessible software tools for designing and customizing physical object
prototypes”” there exists potential for a diverse set of tools for creating ATs for a diverse set of
users”(Hurst & Kane, 2013, p. 638). The authors also described interesting future research
directions of a new accessible tool and how it can be used as a STEM gateway and how
“aesthetic qualities” can create “feeling of ownership” of AT (Hurst & Kane, 2013, p. 638).
	This cluster of research offers ways of thinking about and exploring usable and accessible
DIY making methods. The flaws of DIY-AT listed in this prior research indicate areas to avoid
and techniques to support easier and more accessible DIY making processes. Prior research
underlines the importance of online communities as DIY sharing platforms, which enables my
research to generate additional knowledge sharing techniques that will make use of open
scholarship principles and promote accessibility. This prior research also underlines the
importance of rapid prototyping that is accessible and easy to use in DIY-AT, which informs my
research about the vast need for diversity of DIY making technology that is accessible to
community members.

# Research Questions
“An Exploration of braille letter materiality, form, and interactivity around self-facilitation,
storytelling and DIY concepts in an educational device” is a research creation project that will
test materials to be used in DIY-AT, including LLMs as a material form, with a specific focus on
the domain of braille letter learning. That is, one could argue that LLMs — as experienced as
through material interfaces, and as having outputs embedded in material infrastructures of
learning — can be considered a material form, just like other material components of physical
electronic prototypes. My prototypes provided opportunities to explore open-source DIY
methodologies to address common difficulties in replication and customization. With a focus on
modular design and interchangeable parts, I used rapid prototyping techniques that are available
and accessible to community members and other non-expensive building materials. In the
prototype’s software, I explored the possibilities of LLMs (GPT-3 and GPT-4) working as a self-
themed storytelling agent connected to assistive devices. 

**For this project, I am asking the following questions:**
**Q1:** How can we think about the role that letter and braille cell conceptual knowledge is playing
in assistive devices for early pedagogy for braille?
**Q2:** How can we simultaneously explore the material possibilities of technological prototypes
and simultaneously avoid the pitfalls of technosolutionism?
**Q3:** How can we find ways to use storytelling and task facilitation as a mode to encourage and to inspire self-directed learning?
**Q4:** How should I document my processes to encourage replicability and to break stereotypical
views about the difficulty of DIY making?

# Methodology

## Theoretical Framework
For this project, I adopt an approach that avoids solutionist and technoableist pitfalls and
instead adopts human-centered design, which is aligned with the 10 DJ principles. (Costanza-
Chock, 2020). I research DIY making accessibility flaws in DIY making and document them
through a workbook. The workbook clearly documents the process, drawing from Design
Justice, so its knowledge can be reused and modified to engage with AT making (Costanza-
Chock, 2020). This was a challenge, since my project has a connection to community members,
but their participation is not a planned component in my process.

## Research through Design
RtD is “an approach to conducting scholarly research that employs the methods, practices,
and processes of design practice with the intention of generating new knowledge” (Zimmerman
& Forlizzi, 2014, p. 167). In this work, research and design exploration is formed as an iterative
process, with a critical reflection aspect (both described in more detail in the subsequent section).
(Zimmerman et al., 2007). Every iteration is seen as proposed knowledge outcome and I use
reflection to evaluate further research problems and possibilities, which are key components of
RtD (Zimmerman & Forlizzi, 2014). The knowledge generated from this project is configured
for disabled people and their communities to use and revise it in the future.

## RtD Principles
To show meaningful engagement with RtD practice, I clearly demonstrate the procedures I
use, describe the processes of creation I used, and conclude with reflection, which serves as a
form of knowledge delivery (Bardzell et al., 2016). To ensure the process is well-documented, I
try to create weekly design proposals (Bardzell et al., 2016). For each weekly iteration, the
“protocols, descriptions, and guidelines for its processes, procedures, and activities” were
documented to clearly address the problems and possible solutions (Zimmerman et al., 2010, p.
317). I consistently adhered to this principle; however, there were certain weeks where
exceptions occurred, particularly with problem-solving related to electronic components or when
addressing issues with the 3D printer, which took longer than the weekly timeframe to resolve.
My process clearly outlines my decision-making process, so that each research proposal would
be “explicit about its purpose and about the kinds of problems it can best address" (Zimmerman
et al., 2010, p. 317). To follow RtD and to fulfil my research objectives, I used the following
DIY technologies: 3D printing, electronic parts, microcontrollers (e.g., Arduino) and single-
board computers (e.g., Raspberry PI).

## RtD Knowledge Outcomes
In this project, I present my findings in the form of a workbook, in alignment with RtD
practice (Bardzell et al., 2016). Containing all of the research proposals generated through the
iterative processes, the workbook serves as both documentation and knowledge outcome (Gaver,
2011). Proposals in the workbook are comprised of explanations, instructions, and activities,
researched and work-in-progress, as well as critical reflections (Bardzell et al., 2016;
Zimmerman et al., 2010). It also lists “the project’s way-finding—choices made, breakthroughs,
challenges, and paths not taken” (Bardzell et al., 2016, p. 96). Proposals in the workbook also
should be seen “as contributions to a collection of ideas rather than a representation of final
designs” (Gaver, 2011, p. 1553), in order to leave room for other involvement and facilitate
exploration that goes beyond the eventual outcome.
	Knowledge is produced and disseminated using critical reflections and DIY tutorials. Critical
reflection serves as a final reflective point of each design proposal. These reflections push the
research process forward by generating new questions as knowledge to address in the iterative
process of RtD (Zimmerman et al., 2007). DIY tutorial, which “... document[s],
communicate[s], and disseminate[s] details on the crafting processes, form, and materials of RtD
artifacts” (Desjardins et al., 2017, p. 78). DIY tutorials “concisely and clearly communicat[e]
how to make a wide range of things” which is a knowledge outcome as well as a help to enrich
the open scholarship approach (Desjardins et al., 2017, p. 78).
